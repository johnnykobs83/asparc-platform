Training · 01

The Warm-Up

Every session and match starts the same way. A good warm-up lifts performance, lowers injury risk and gets heads switched on.

Perform

Light aerobic work, dynamic movement and football-specific drills improve sprinting, jumping and kicking. Warmer muscles and more blood flow do most of the work.

Protect

Strength, balance and mobility work built into the warm-up and repeated all season reduces strains, sprains and other non-contact injuries.

Prepare

Passing, control and shooting settle players on the ball. Possession drills can rehearse the tactical idea for the day.

In practice
  • Run the Perform+ running block first, then mobility, ball work and speed, in that order.
  • Adjust the content for weather, individual players and the session ahead. Never change the structure.
  • The full warm-up, with a phase planner by age group and an on-pitch run-through, is in The ASPARC Warm-Up.

Training · 02

Periodisation

Planning training so players keep improving and peak for matches. The year is broken into season phases, multi-week blocks and match-to-match cycles, each with its own technical, tactical and physical focus.

Supercompensation

Each session causes fatigue and a short dip in readiness. With enough recovery the body adapts past where it started. Train again too early and fatigue piles up. Leave it too long and the gains fade. Getting dose and recovery right is the whole game.

Football's demands

Players need speed, power, endurance and strength, all planned alongside tactical work. Building conditioning into technical and tactical drills saves time. No single model suits every team.

Progressive overload

The body adapts only when pushed past what it is used to. Change load (intensity × volume) week to week. Without progression, players plateau or lose fitness.

Specificity

Cycling and swimming build an engine but won't make a player faster over 10 metres. Small-sided games (3v3, 4v4) overload technical, tactical and physical demands together. Large-sided games (8v8 to 11v11) overload high-speed running and sprinting. Top-up running should match each player's position.

Individuality

Players respond differently because of training history, maturation and genetics. Run a simple needs analysis: list the qualities your position needs with the coach, rate yourself, target the biggest gaps, and re-rate through the season. Testing gives objective numbers.

Recovery

Adaptation happens during recovery. Avoid heavy days back to back and vary the physical focus. Amateur and youth players carry extra load from school, work and family, so plan for it. Finish each block with an unload week.

Example block · 4v4 small-sided games, intensity held constant
Week 14 × 2′ · 8′
Week 25 × 2′ · 10′
Week 36 × 2′ · 12′
Week 43 × 2′ · 6′

Add a repetition each week, then lengthen the bouts. The unload week keeps intensity but halves the volume, then loading starts again.

In practice
  • Coaches plan each match week for recovery, conditioning and match preparation, and set it inside the season plan.
  • End every 3–4 week block with an unload week at about 50% volume.
  • Use each player's testing results to set individual top-up running.

Training · 03

Strength Training

Jumping, kicking, sprinting and winning duels all come back to strength and power. Strength work on and off the pitch improves them and helps prevent common injuries in youth and adult players.

Getting started

Football Australia's FUNdamentals and Perform+ programs are the entry point. Beyond that, programs should come from qualified people: ESSA-accredited exercise or sports scientists and ASCA-accredited strength and conditioning coaches.

Young players

Strength training is safe for children and adolescents when it is designed and supervised properly. It supports long-term development, bone and metabolic health and wellbeing. Start with bodyweight, bands, medicine balls or light bars. Technique comes first, then volume, then load. A good coach checks maturation, training age, technique and current strength before setting a start point.

Progressive overload

Volume is sets × reps, intensity is the weight, load is both. Raise it gradually.

Specificity

Pick gym exercises that look like on-pitch actions, such as squats or box jumps for jumping and sprinting.

Individuality

Training history, maturation and sex all change the response. Select exercises and loads per player.

Training · 04

Recovery

National team players often cover more than 11 km a match, with up to a tenth of it above 20 km/h. Youth players from U15 up can hit peak intensities close to professionals. Recovering well is what lets them train and play again.

Why players get tired

  • Dehydration. In heat, losses reach 2% of body mass, about 1.2 kg for a 60 kg player. Smaller losses already hurt endurance.
  • Low fuel. Muscle and liver glycogen run down late in matches, limiting sprints, jumps and tackles.
  • Muscle damage. Sprinting, cutting, jumping and kicking cause soreness and swelling, especially early in pre-season.
  • Mental fatigue. Constantly reading ball, teammates and opponents is draining and can affect physical output.

How long it takes

One match a week leaves time to recover and train. Matches less than four days apart may not allow full recovery, which lowers performance and raises injury risk. Fluid balance comes back within hours. Soreness peaks 24–48 hours after a match and can last up to 4 days.

Sleep habits

  • Go to bed when sleepy. If you can't sleep, do something calm until you are.
  • Keep the same wake time every day, weekends included.
  • Naps under an hour and before 3 pm.
  • Phones and laptops out of bed. Bedroom quiet, dark and cool.
  • No caffeine after lunch and no hard exercise right before bed.

Other methods

  • Active recovery. No clear proof it speeds recovery. If you use it, do 15+ min of easy swim, bike or jog the day after, not straight after the match.
  • Ice baths. Evidence is mixed but most players feel better. Get out early if uncomfortable. Skip them in pre-season as they may blunt adaptation.
  • Stretching. Doesn't reduce soreness, but still useful for range of motion.
In practice
  • Weigh in before and after matches in minimal clothing and use the fluid calculator in the hydration section.
  • Have a carb + protein drink ready in the change room for full time.
  • Schedule recovery sessions for the day after the match, and leave ice baths out of pre-season.

Training · 05

Extreme Environments

Heat, cold and altitude all change how the body copes with football. An Australian season can bring the first two within a few months. Planning ahead prevents most of the problems.

Heat

The body runs best near 37 °C. Muscles make heat during play, and in hot, humid conditions it can't escape, so core temperature can pass 40 °C. That puts players, coaches and spectators at risk of heat illness.

  • Acclimatise over days to weeks. The first hot days of spring catch players out.
  • Start hydrated, sip throughout, add drinks breaks.
  • Stay out of the sun before the warm-up. Cool down between warm-up and kick-off, at half-time and after: ice towels, fans, ice baths, slushies.
  • Follow the Football Australia heat policy, which uses Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT).
Cold

Less common than heat illness, but long exposure can lead to hypothermia. Cold air, wind and wet kit all speed up heat loss.

  • Keep moving on the pitch.
  • Layer up and shelter during breaks.
  • Keep drinking. Players still sweat in the cold even when they don't feel thirsty.
  • Cold can aggravate asthma and other breathing problems. Parents and coaches should watch for it.
Altitude

Rare for Australian teams, but national sides have played at Kunming (about 1,900 m). Below 2,000 m is generally safe, but lower air pressure reduces oxygen uptake and endurance.

  • The ball flies differently, making crosses and saves harder to judge.
  • The best fix is to acclimatise well before the match.
In practice
  • Check the forecast and WBGT before every summer session and apply the heat policy for drinks and cooling breaks.
  • On cold nights, extend the warm-up and keep subs in layers and moving.
  • Players with asthma bring their medication to every session.

Training · 06

Exercise Testing

Outpacing an opponent, jumping higher or still running at the 85th minute can decide a match. Testing isolates those qualities so we can see where each player stands and whether training is working.

What testing tells us

  • A player's physical strengths and weaknesses.
  • Whether a program is improving the qualities it targets.
  • Whether a player meets the physical demands of their position and the team's style of play.

Testing safely and fairly

  • Safety. Test under supervision, after a full warm-up. Adults complete a pre-exercise screening first.
  • Standardise. Same set-up, time of day, days since last match, surface, weather and warm-up every time.
  • Read results kindly. Growth and maturation change scores a lot. Most players make up for one weakness with other strengths.
Endurance

Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test Level 1

Repeated 2 × 20 m shuttles at speeds set by audio beeps, with 10 seconds of active recovery between each. The pace rises until the player misses the line twice. Score is total distance covered.

Power

Standing Broad Jump

From a two-foot stance behind the line, swing the arms and jump as far forward as possible, landing on both feet. Measure to the back of the nearest heel. Best of three attempts.

In practice
  • Test at the start of pre-season, mid-season and end of season, with the same protocol each time.
  • Results feed each player's needs analysis and individual top-up work. Youth results are always read alongside maturation.
  • Keep results in one place so players can track their own progress over time.

Health · 07

Nutrition Foundations

Social media and pro players' diets make nutrition look complicated. It isn't. Like a good training program, the basics done every day do most of the work.

Find your why

Know what you want food to do for you. Better performance, more energy when you wake up, better mood, fewer injuries, a stronger immune system, healthy growth. Your reason decides which habits matter most.

Boulders and pebbles

Daily habits are the boulders: regular meals, enough food, enough fluid, plenty of colour. Timing tricks and supplements are pebbles. Only think about pebbles once the boulders are automatic.

In practice
  • Junior players: food first, no supplements.
  • Adult players considering a supplement should speak to a sports dietitian first and use batch-tested products only.
Health · 08

Fuelling Enough

Players need enough energy for daily life, growing and training. It is the most overlooked part of performance nutrition, especially on busy school, work and travel days.

Signs you may be under-fuelling

  • A schedule so busy that meals get missed.
  • Feeling you have to train to "earn" food.
  • Long gaps between meals and snacks, or skipped meals.
  • Being very hungry or feeling out of control with food by the end of the day.

Why eating enough matters

  • Lower injury risk.
  • Sharper concentration and decisions.
  • Better adaptation to training.
  • Growth and strong bones.

Weight can affect performance, but it doesn't define a player's success, worth or identity. The aim is to fuel for your goals and be the best footballer you can be. These signs are common, but they are not normal.

In practice
  • Coaches don't comment on players' body weight or shape.
  • If you think you or a teammate might be under-eating, speak to a sports doctor or sports dietitian.
Health · 09

Protein: Total, Type, Timing

Protein provides the amino acids that build and repair muscle, support bone and make hormones. Most players get enough from food without supplements.

Total

Training increases muscle turnover, so players need more than the general population: about 1.5–2.2 g per kg of body weight each day.

Type

Animal proteins carry all essential amino acids in high amounts. Plant-based eaters should mix sources: beans, lentils, nuts and seeds, not only tofu. Leucine triggers muscle rebuilding. Good sources: milk, Greek yoghurt, cottage cheese, soy milk, eggs, lean meat.

Timing

Don't save it all for dinner. Spread portions of at least 20 g across 3–4 meals or snacks, and have a protein-rich snack within 30–60 minutes of a key session.

Health · 10

Recovery Nutrition

Turning up energised and strong builds confidence. Recovery is one of the things in your control, and it is more than a protein shake.

Refuel

Training burns carbohydrate from blood and muscle, faster at higher intensity. Low stores bring fatigue and soreness. Bread, rice, pasta, fruit, cereal, muesli, yoghurt, milk.

Repair

Small tears in muscle fibres are how you get stronger. Protein supplies the building blocks. Meat, chicken, fish, eggs, tofu, tempeh, legumes, yoghurt, milk.

Rehydrate

Replace the fluid and electrolytes lost in sweat to help energy, focus and concentration. Sweat rates differ person to person, so check your urine colour.

Revitalise

Colourful foods help manage inflammation and support immunity. Berries in a smoothie, extra veg at dinner, salad in your lunch wrap.

< 12 hto the next session? Start refuelling straight away
24–48 hthe real recovery window when the next session is a day or more away

The 30-minute window matters most when you train or play again within 12 hours. Otherwise, focus on what you eat across the whole next day or two. Players who train most days are always recovering.

Health · 11

Hydration

Hydration sits alongside eating, training and sleeping as a base for performance. Well-hydrated players make decisions more easily, focus better, find sessions easier and get fewer stomach upsets.

Why players dehydrate

Sweat cools the body but costs fluid and electrolytes. How much depends on genetics, sex, size, fitness and climate, and it changes over time. Heat and humidity increase losses. Heat-acclimatised players sweat more but lose fewer electrolytes. Feeling flat and finding everything harder are common signs.

What to drink

  • Water first. Cheap, portable, and it covers most daily needs. Soups and other fluids count too.
  • Electrolytes for heavy sweating and hot days. They help absorb fluid but carry no carbohydrate.
  • Sports drinks when you need fluid and fuel: hard sessions, games, and very hot or humid days.

If you don't like water

  • Use a bottle you like, add ice, or infuse it with fruit or iced tea bags.
  • Tie drinking to habits: a glass with every meal and snack, bottle in hand with your phone and keys.
  • Make it easy so it happens without thinking.
